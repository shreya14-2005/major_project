4. Scientific Evaluation of Label Verification Status
-----------------------------------------------------
- Biological Nutrient Label Status: UNVERIFIED.
- Basis of Annotation: The dataset contributor states that the photographs were "taken during field surveys led by domain experts". Annotations were assigned based on visual observation of leaf symptoms in the field.
- Absence of Laboratory Tissue Analysis: No records exist indicating destructive laboratory foliar testing—such as Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES) for mineral micronutrients/secondary nutrients, Kjeldahl digestion for total nitrogen, or spectrophotometric phosphomolybdate assays for phosphorus.
- Absence of Controlled Deprivation Trials: The plants were not grown under nutrient-controlled nutrient solution cultures (e.g., Hoagland solution minus N, P, K, or Mg) where symptoms could be definitively tied to single nutrient omissions.
- Absence of Soil Analysis Records: No corresponding soil test logs (cation exchange capacity, available nitrogen, Bray-P/Olsen-P, exchangeable potassium, or soil pH) are attached to individual image entries.

5. Risk of Color/Symptom Confounding
------------------------------------
- Symptom Overlap: Chlorosis (yellowing), purpling (anthocyanin accumulation), marginal necrosis, and stunted leaf margins are physiological stress responses that can be triggered by multiple overlapping factors:
  * Nitrogen vs. Sulfur vs. Water Stress: General leaf yellowing can indicate nitrogen deficiency, sulfur deficiency, root lodging, drought stress, or herbicide injury.
  * Phosphorus vs. Cold/Genetics: Purpling of leaf margins can arise from phosphorus deficiency, cold soil temperatures inhibiting root nutrient uptake, or normal hybrid-specific anthocyanin expression.
  * Magnesium vs. Viral/Fungal Interveinal Damage: Interveinal chlorosis on lower leaves is characteristic of magnesium deficiency but can be mimicked by early stages of viral striping or root nematode injury.
  * Potassium vs. Salinity/Bacterial Edge Burn: Marginal leaf scorching (firing) can be confused with salt toxicity, wind burn, or bacterial leaf scorch.
- Rule on Leaf Color Inference: Nutrient deficiencies CANNOT and MUST NOT be scientifically inferred from leaf color alone. Visual inspection provides diagnostic cues, but confirmation requires chemical foliar tissue analysis.
